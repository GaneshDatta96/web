The Decision Cost Framework

Ganesh Datta · code 100705 · 10 Aug 2026 · source page

By Ganesh R Datta. I install sales infrastructure for coaches and founders. This page is the source declaration of a method I named, not a certificate from someone else.

The Decision Cost Framework is my original method for a simple question: what does it cost to keep deciding this by hand? Decision Cost is the organizational cost of using human judgment to start, approve, prioritize, interpret, escalate, or authorize work when Automate, Assist, or Keep Human would be a better split. A Decision Cost Audit is the engagement that inventories one lane and ships one measured change. v0.1. Declared. Not certified.

The one-line idea

Most teams ask: can AI do this? That question is too wide. It produces a chat window and the same human still approving the output. The better question is: what does it cost to keep deciding this by hand?

I kept hitting that in sales ops. Follow-up waiting on the founder. The next lead picked from memory. Status assembled in a spreadsheet so someone could decide if the week was okay. Tools were present. Judgment was still the router. I needed a name for that tax so I could stop arguing about software and start arguing about allocation.

That name is Decision Cost. The method is the Decision Cost Framework. The paid working pass is a Decision Cost Audit.

What Decision Cost is

Decision Cost is the organizational cost of using human judgment to start, approve, prioritize, interpret, escalate, or authorize work. That includes time, attention, delay, error risk, coordination, and the opportunity cost of that judgment, when a better Automate / Assist / Keep Human split exists.

It is not all labor. Typing a recap is work. Deciding whether the recap needs a human, who sends it, and whether the exception is real: that is Decision Cost. The unit is a Decision Episode. Something triggers. A person chooses or authorizes, or hunts for enough information to choose. Work moves, waits, or escalates.

A four-minute decision done twenty times a day by three people is not a four-minute problem. It is hours of judgment load, plus interruption residue, plus queue delay for everyone waiting on those calls, plus the messy calls tired people make at 6pm.

What it is not

It is not payroll. It is not every minute on a timesheet. It is not a new word for automation consulting. Automation is one treatment. The diagnosis is where judgment is spent, including what must stay human.

It is not transaction cost economics with a fresh coat of paint. Coase and Williamson are about the cost of using markets and governing exchange. Decision Cost is mostly the intrafirm cousin: internal coordination and judgment. I cite TCE. I do not claim I invented it.

It is not cognitive load either. Load is an input. Decision Cost converts that load into organizational consequence: delay, error, queue, senior attention burned on routing. Adjacent research is real. The named method, the three scores, and the audit sequence are mine.

The three public scores

I reject a single mega-multiplicative vanity number. Frequency times time times complexity times switching times consequence times headcount explodes, double-counts, and looks precise. Three readable scores beat one fake trophy.

Automate, Assist, Keep Human

Automate means a machine owns it under clear rules. Same inputs, stable policy, cheap reversal, low need for a person in the loop.

Assist means AI or software drafts. A human decides. Retrieval, first drafts, triage suggestions, recaps. The judgment stays with a named person.

Keep Human is mandatory for some work. Ambiguity, accountability, ethics, relationships, strategy, irreversible calls. If everything is Automate, you are fantasizing. If everything is Keep Human, you are protecting comfort, not judgment.

Automation is not automatically better. Reliability and task difficulty jointly decide whether a machine helps or just creates a babysitting job. Any new babysitting time goes back into Load. I count it.

How a Decision Cost Audit runs

Human-led interview. Structured data. AI draft. Human lock. One quick win. Measurement. Then, if it worked, a case narrative.

The rule is not optional: AI never ships a recommendation without human sign-off. A chatbot that dumps "automate this" on your calendar is not an audit.

What you get:

  • Top 5 Automate now
  • Top 5 Assist
  • Keep Human, mandatory
  • One quick win, implemented
  • Before/after measure, including babysitting time
  • 30-day recommendations

I run it on one lane. Sales follow-up is the lane I see most, because that is the work I already install. Content publishing, support triage, and ops reporting work the same way. I will not audit your whole company in a week. That produces a slogan.

Free self-assessment

Pick one workflow lane. Answer for that lane only. 0 = rarely / no. 1 = sometimes. 2 = often / yes. Total out of 20. This teaches the lens. It is not the audit.

  1. The same type of approval or choice repeats daily or weekly with little new information.
  2. Work frequently waits on a person even when the right answer is usually obvious.
  3. People search across multiple tools before they can act.
  4. Follow-ups depend on someone remembering rather than a system rule.
  5. Priorities are re-decided from scratch instead of using a standing rubric.
  6. Status is assembled manually, spreadsheet or Slack archaeology, to decide if things are okay.
  7. Different people make inconsistent calls on similar cases.
  8. Exceptions are handled by pinging the same human router.
  9. AI or chat tools are already used ad hoc, but outputs still need the same human cleanup every time.
  10. At least one recurring decision is annoying, frequent, low-stakes, and probably rule-based.
ScoreMeaningNext step
0–6Decision Cost may be modest in this laneWatch a week. Try another lane.
7–12Material recurring judgment loadList your top 8 recurring decisions. Mark Automate / Assist / Keep Human as gut calls.
13–20High likelihood of wasted decision capacityRun a Decision Cost Audit on this lane. Implement one measured quick win.

Now write five recurring decisions. Mark A, S, or H. If they are all A, you are over-automating in your head. If they are all H, you may be protecting comfort, not judgment.

Open versus closed

Open: the definition, Automate / Assist / Keep Human, this self-assessment, and case narratives once they exist.

Closed: full probes, scoring thresholds, recommendation logic, the quality bar on templates, override datasets.
