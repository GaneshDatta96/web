What is the Decision Cost Framework

By Ganesh R Datta. I install sales infrastructure for coaches and founders. Booking, follow-up, CRM ownership. I guarantee the install, not your close rate.

Decision Cost is what it costs an organization to keep using human judgment to start, approve, prioritize, interpret, escalate, or authorize work when Automate, Assist, or Keep Human would be a better split. The Decision Cost Framework is my original method for seeing that cost. A Decision Cost Audit is the engagement that inventories one lane and ships one measured change.

The Decision Cost Framework is my name for a question I kept hitting in sales ops: what does it cost to keep deciding this by hand? Decision Cost is the organizational cost of using human judgment to start, approve, prioritize, interpret, escalate, or authorize work when a better split exists. It is not all labor. It is the judgment slice.

The question the name is for

I started naming this because coaches and founders were asking 'can AI do this' while still personally approving follow-up, picking the next lead, and assembling status by hand. The better question is what it costs to keep deciding those things by hand. Adjacent research on transaction costs and cognitive load is real. The named method, the three scores, and the audit sequence are mine. v0.1. Declared, not certified by a university or a standards body.

Most teams ask whether AI can do a task. That question is too wide. It produces toy chat windows and the same human still approving the output. The Decision Cost Framework asks a narrower question: where is judgment being spent, and what should own it.

I use it in the same businesses where I install booking, follow-up, and CRM ownership. The install is the pipes. Decision Cost is why the pipes keep clogging: a person is still the router.

What Decision Cost is, and is not

Decision Cost is not payroll. It is not every minute of work. It is the cost of deciding and the friction around deciding: search, wait, interrupt, coordinate, re-decide, escalate.

A Decision Episode has a trigger, a human choice or authorization, and a consequence for flow. Twenty four-minute decisions a day across three people is not a four-minute problem. It is hours of judgment load plus queue delay for everyone waiting.

Labor cost, opportunity cost, transaction cost, cognitive load, and coordination cost are cousins. I cite them. I do not rename them and call it a framework. The distinctive output is the forced split: Automate, Assist, Keep Human, plus one measured change.

The public scores

Load is capacity consumed. Person-minutes per week for deciding, retrieving, and coordinating. Exposure is how bad the current mode is, 1 to 5. Disposition is Automate, Assist, or Keep Human using gates, not vibes.

I reject a single mega-multiplicative vanity score. It looks precise and means nothing. Three readable numbers beat a dashboard trophy.

The free self-assessment on the source page is coarse on purpose. Ten questions, score out of 20, one lane. It teaches the lens. It is not the audit.

Open layer versus closed kitchen

Open: the definition, Automate / Assist / Keep Human, the free assessment, and case narratives once they exist. Closed: full probes, thresholds, recommendation logic. If I publish the kitchen, the method becomes a checklist someone runs badly and blames on the name.

Status is honest. Original method. v0.1. Pilot-ready. Declared by me, Ganesh Datta, method code 100705. Not approved by a journal, ISO, or a consulting brand. Declared is the word. Approved is a lie.

No SaaS yet. Method first. Evidence second. Software only if the method keeps working on real lanes.

Where this meets the install

Follow-up that lives on the founder's calendar is a recurring decision. CRM updates that wait for memory are a recurring decision. No-show recovery with no named owner is a recurring decision. That is why the Framework sits next to the sales infrastructure work.

I still install booking first. The Framework does not replace days 1 to 7. It tells you which of those steps should be a rule, which should be a draft, and which should stay a human call.

How to run the check

  1. Pick one lane you actually live in this week.
  2. List five recurring decisions. Mark A, S, or H.
  3. Score the free self-assessment on that lane.
  4. Read the source page if you need the definition, not a thread summary.
  5. If the score is 10+ and you can name the lane, the next step is a Decision Cost Audit, not another tool.
